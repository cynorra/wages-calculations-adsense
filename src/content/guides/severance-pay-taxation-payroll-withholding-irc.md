Severance pay is money an employer pays when employment ends. No federal law requires it for most employees, so it exists by contract, policy, or negotiation. But once it is paid, the tax rules are firm: it is **wages**, and it is taxed more like a final paycheck than like a gift or a settlement.

## Three tax layers

| Tax | Treatment of severance |
| :--- | :--- |
| Federal income tax | Included in gross income; employer withholds |
| Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) | **Applies.** The Supreme Court held in *United States v. Quality Stores* (2014) that severance is "wages" under FICA |
| Additional Medicare tax (0.9%) | Employer withholds on wages over $200,000 in the calendar year |
| State income tax | Generally taxed; state supplemental rates may apply |
| Unemployment tax (FUTA/SUTA) | Generally applies to severance in the year paid |

The Social Security portion stops once the employee's wages for the year reach the annual wage base ($176,100 in 2025, and indexed upward each year). An executive who has already earned the base in the year will see only Medicare withheld on severance.

## Lump sum vs. salary continuation

How the severance is paid changes how it is withheld.

| Payment form | Withholding method |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Lump sum** paid separately from regular pay | Supplemental wages: employer may use the flat 22% rate (37% for amounts over $1 million in the year) or the aggregate method |
| **Salary continuation** paid on regular paydays | Treated as regular wages; withheld using the employee's Form W-4 and the normal tables |
| **Accrued PTO** paid at separation | Wages; may be supplemental depending on how it is paid; some states require immediate payment |

## A worked example

An employee with a $90,000 salary is laid off with a **$30,000 lump-sum** severance payment. Her earlier wages that year do not exceed the Social Security base, so all FICA applies. Using the flat 22% federal supplemental rate:

| Item | Calculation | Amount |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gross severance | | $30,000.00 |
| Federal income tax withholding | 22% × $30,000 | −$6,600.00 |
| Social Security | 6.2% × $30,000 | −$1,860.00 |
| Medicare | 1.45% × $30,000 | −$435.00 |
| **Net before state tax** | | **$21,105.00** |

$$\text{Net} = \$30{,}000 - (0.22 + 0.062 + 0.0145) \times \$30{,}000 = \$21{,}105$$

State withholding would reduce this further. But the 22% rate is only a *prepayment*. If her real marginal rate is 12% or 24%, the difference settles on her Form 1040 as a refund or additional tax due. Withholding is not the final tax.

If the same $30,000 were paid as salary continuation over six months, the monthly $5,000 would be withheld under her W-4 like any paycheck, which often yields a *smaller* withholding rate than 22%, but the tax liability for the year is computed the same.

## The release and the clock

Most severance is offered in exchange for a **release of claims**. If the employee is 40 or older, the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act sets the process: the employee must be given **21 days to consider** (45 days if the severance is part of a group layoff program) and **7 days to revoke** after signing. Payment generally cannot start until the revocation period ends. This affects when the payroll tax obligation arises, because wages are taxed when paid.

## Section 409A: the timing trap

Severance paid on a schedule can be **deferred compensation** under Internal Revenue Code § 409A, which imposes a 20% additional tax on the employee for noncompliant plans. Two exceptions let most severance avoid 409A:

- **Short-term deferral.** Paid by March 15 of the year after the year in which the right vests.
- **Separation pay plan.** Involuntary separation only, with total payments limited to two times the lesser of annual compensation or the § 401(a)(17) limit, and paid by the end of the second year following separation.

Large executive packages with installments, or severance tied to a voluntary resignation for "good reason," deserve a § 409A review before signing.

## Unemployment benefits

Severance does not automatically bar unemployment, but **state rules vary**. Some states treat severance as wages that reduce or delay benefits for the period it covers. Others ignore it if it was paid as a lump sum. Employees should ask their state agency about timing before filing.

## Checklist for payroll

- Pay lump sums as a separately identified supplemental payment so the 22% method can be used.
- Apply Social Security and Medicare to all severance, up to the wage base for Social Security.
- Report severance in W-2 boxes 1, 3, and 5.
- Confirm the release and revocation period before releasing payment.
- Check the state's rules for final pay timing and accrued vacation.

## IRC Section 409A Nonqualified Deferred Compensation Exposure

When structuring executive and managerial severance packages, corporate counsel and payroll directors must ensure strict compliance with Internal Revenue Code Section 409A (26 U.S.C. § 409A). Severance arrangements that extend payments across multiple tax years or tie distributions to non-compete milestones risk classification as nonqualified deferred compensation:

- **The Separation Pay Exemption:** Under Treasury Regulation § 1.409A-1(b)(9)(iii), severance payments are exempt from Section 409A if paid upon involuntary termination, do not exceed two times the employee's annual compensation (or two times the IRC § 401(a)(17) limit, whichever is less), and are fully disbursed by the end of the second calendar year following separation.
- **Penalties for Non-Compliance:** If a severance agreement violates Section 409A, the employee faces immediate income taxation on all vested amounts, an additional **20% statutory federal excise penalty**, and IRS interest penalties calculated at the federal underpayment rate plus 1%.

Employers must structure severance release agreements with clear execution windows to ensure all payments satisfy the short-term deferral or separation pay safe harbors.
