## Dual-Agency Payroll Record Retention Mandates Under FLSA and Internal Revenue Code

Employers subject to federal wage-and-hour laws must navigate a dual-agency regulatory framework governed by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) and the Internal Revenue Code (IRC). Under 29 C.F.R. § 516.5, primary payroll records containing employee identifying information, hourly pay rates, daily and weekly hours worked, total earnings, and itemized deductions must be retained for a minimum of three years. Supplementary records—including original daily time cards, piece-rate tickets, work schedules, wage-rate tables, and records of additions to or deductions from wages—fall under 29 C.F.R. § 516.6 and require a minimum two-year retention period. Concurrently, Internal Revenue Service (IRS) regulations under 26 C.F.R. § 31.6001-1 and IRS Publication 15 require employers to preserve all employment tax records for at least four years after the date the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later. Because regulatory authority is split between the U.S. Department of Labor (DOL) and the IRS, maintaining records solely to satisfy the shortest federal retention threshold exposes an enterprise to severe legal liability during statutory audits or private enforcement litigation.

## Interplay Between the FLSA Statute of Limitations and The Mt. Clemens Evidentiary Burden Shift

A common operational error in payroll administration is discarding raw time cards exactly two years after creation, as permitted by 29 C.F.R. § 516.6. While this practice complies with the literal language of the recordkeeping regulation, it creates substantial legal vulnerability under the FLSA enforcement framework. Under 29 U.S.C. § 255(a), the statute of limitations for recovering back wages under the FLSA is two years for ordinary violations, but extends to three years if the violation is determined to be "willful"—meaning the employer knew or showed reckless disregard for whether its conduct violated the statute (*McLaughlin v. Richland Shoe Co.*, 486 U.S. 128 (1988)). 

When an employee files a collective action alleging unpaid overtime covering a three-year lookback period, an employer that destroyed time cards from year three (retaining only the summary payroll registers required by 29 C.F.R. § 516.5) faces the evidentiary burden-shifting framework established in *Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680 (1946). Under the *Mt. Clemens* doctrine, if an employer fails to produce accurate, complete daily time records, the employee satisfies their initial burden of proof by presenting credible testimony estimating the hours worked without pay. 

Once the employee establishes this prima facie case, the burden shifts entirely to the employer to submit evidence of the precise amount of work performed, or to negate the reasonableness of the inference drawn from the employee's evidence. Summary payroll registers showing only total paid hours do not refute an employee's assertion of unrecorded off-the-clock work. Without daily time cards or electronic log files to disprove the claim, courts adopt the employee's estimated damages calculation:

$$ \text{Estimated Unpaid Overtime Liability} = \sum_{w=1}^{156} \left( \text{Claimed Weekly Off-the-Clock Hours}_w \times 1.5 \times \text{Regular Rate}_w \right) $$

If the employer misclassified employees under the [/guides/salary-exempt-vs-non-exempt-flsa-test/](/guides/salary-exempt-vs-non-exempt-flsa-test/) criteria, the absence of time cards covering the full three-year statutory window leaves the enterprise defenceless against double damages (liquidated damages equal to 100% of back pay) under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b).

## Reconciling the Two-Year FLSA Time Card Rule with the Four-Year IRS Employment Tax Mandate

A tension exists between FLSA rules governing supplementary payroll records and IRS regulations governing employment taxes. Under 26 C.F.R. § 31.6001-1, employers must maintain all records pertaining to employment taxes for a minimum of four years after the tax becomes due or is paid. This rule applies not merely to summary tax filings like IRS Form 941, but to all underlying documentation used to determine the taxable wage base.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                     FEDERAL PAYROLL RECORD RETENTION TIMEFRAMES                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                                                                                   |
|  2 YEARS                  3 YEARS                       4 YEARS                   |
|  (29 C.F.R. § 516.6)      (29 C.F.R. § 516.5)           (26 C.F.R. § 31.6001-1)   |
|  ------------------      ------------------            -----------------------    |
|  - Time Cards             - Payroll Registers           - Form 941 & Form 940      |
|  - Work Schedules         - Employee Identifiers        - Form W-4 Certificates    |
|  - Wage-Rate Tables       - Total Hours Worked          - Fringe Benefit Logs      |
|  - Additions/Deductions   - Regular Pay Rates           - Tip Allocation Records   |
|                           - DB / Prevailing Wage        - Taxable Wage Adjustments |
|                                                                                   |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

When wage additions or deductions are made—such as lodging credits under FLSA section 3(m), meal allowances, tip credit allocations, or discretionary non-cash fringe benefits—the underlying daily records serve a dual purpose. They function both as wage-and-hour compliance artifacts under 29 C.F.R. § 516.6 and as tax substantiation files under IRC § 6001. 

If an employer purges raw time cards or meal-period deduction logs after two years, and the IRS subsequently conducts an employment tax audit in year four for tax periods occurring in 2022 or 2023, the employer cannot substantiate wage exclusions or non-taxable meal deductions. The IRS auditor may reclassify non-taxable wage additions as taxable compensation under IRC § 3121(a), assessing unpaid Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) taxes, Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA) liabilities, and mandatory interest penalties. 

Similarly, when managing [/guides/supplemental-wage-withholding-bonus-taxation-rules/](/guides/supplemental-wage-withholding-bonus-taxation-rules/), employers must maintain the underlying supplemental pay authorization records, commission tables, and allocation formulas for at least four years to verify proper flat-rate withholding tiers (22% standard, 37% for supplemental wages exceeding $1 million) against IRS payroll tax audits.

## Multi-State Compliance Conflicts and Extended Statutory Retention Horizons

Federal retention periods set a national baseline, but state statutes frequently mandate significantly longer retention windows. State wage payment laws and unfair competition statutes routinely subject employers to statutory audit windows that outlast FLSA and IRS minimums.

```
               SUMMARY OF RETENTION HORIZONS BY JURISDICTION
               
               Federal FLSA (Time Cards):      2 Years
               Federal FLSA (Payroll Master):  3 Years
               Federal IRS (Tax Files):        4 Years
               California Labor Code § 1174:   3 Years (4 Years under UCL)
               New York Labor Law § 195(4):    6 Years
```

### New York Labor Law Compliance
Under New York Labor Law (NYLL) § 195(4), every employer must establish, maintain, and preserve basic payroll records—including gross wages, daily and weekly hours worked, pay rates, deductions, and net wages—for a minimum of **six years**. The NYLL statute of limitations for wage claims (NYLL § 198(3)) matches this six-year period. Consequently, maintaining time cards or payroll ledgers for only two or three years in New York constitutes an automatic recordkeeping violation under NYLL § 218, creating an immediate presumption in favor of the employee's wage estimates.

### California Labor Code and Unfair Competition Law
California Labor Code § 1174 requires employers to retain itemized payroll records showing daily hours worked and wage rates for at least three years. However, claims for unpaid overtime or missed meal/rest break premiums are regularly brought under the California Unfair Competition Law (UCL), California Business and Professions Code § 17208, which carries a **four-year statute of limitations**. Employers that discard time cards prior to the four-year mark are left without documentary defenses against employee claims for wage penalties.

### Federal Public Works and Prevailing Wage Rules
Under the Davis-Bacon Act and related acts (29 C.F.R. § 5.5(a)(3)), contracts subject to prevailing wage determinations mandate that certified payroll records (Form WH-347 or equivalent) and basic payroll records be retained for three years after project completion. For detailed mechanics regarding certified payroll submissions, see [/guides/prevailing-wage-davis-bacon-act-compliance/](/guides/prevailing-wage-davis-bacon-act-compliance/).

## Employer Statutory Audit Compliance Protocols

To maintain regulatory compliance across federal, state, and tax jurisdictions, payroll controllers and legal counsel should apply the following baseline compliance rules:

1. **Adopt a Unified Six-Year Retention Standard**  
   Discarding records according to disparate two-, three-, and four-year statutory minimums creates operational complexity and evidentiary risk. Establishing a company-wide policy to retain all time cards, raw badge-swipe data, payroll registers, wage rate tables, and tax filings for **six full calendar years plus the current calendar year** satisfies FLSA, IRS, Davis-Bacon, and state standards (such as NYLL).

2. **Secure Electronic Storage Parameters Under 29 C.F.R. § 516.1**  
   Electronic recordkeeping is permissible under federal regulations provided the system offers:
   * Microfilm, digital image, or database retrieval capabilities that allow immediate inspection upon request by DOL Wage and Hour Division (WHD) investigators.
   * Legible conversion functionality to print hard-copy records or export structured flat files (e.g., CSV or XML format).
   * Redundant backup procedures that prevent data corruption or loss during systems migrations.

3. **Retain Form W-4 and Withholding Documentation Post-Termination**  
   Preserve IRS Form W-4, state tax withholding certificates, and direct deposit authorizations for **four years after the date of employee termination**, rather than four years from the date of form execution.

4. **Document Wage Addition and Deduction Justifications**  
   Maintain all records establishing the actual cost of board, lodging, or other facilities furnished under FLSA section 3(m) (29 C.F.R. Part 531) for four years to survive joint DOL and IRS audits.

5. **Maintain Time Modification Logs**  
   Ensure modern electronic time-and-attendance systems generate immutable audit logs detailing every supervisor edit, manual time entry, or schedule override. Deleting raw clock punches while retaining only corrected manager totals violates 29 C.F.R. § 516.6(a)(1) and triggers adverse evidentiary inferences during litigation.
