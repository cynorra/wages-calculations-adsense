## Statutory Framework of Reporting Time Pay vs. FLSA Preemption

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., imposes no obligation upon private-sector employers to compensate non-exempt employees for "reporting time" or "show-up" pay when a scheduled shift is canceled or truncated upon arrival. Federal law characterizes hours worked solely by the performance of compensable labor; consequently, time spent in transit or waiting at the worksite for a shift that does not occur is generally non-compensable under 29 C.F.R. § 785.7. However, this federal silence creates a regulatory vacuum filled by state-specific mandates in jurisdictions such as California, New York, and Massachusetts. These state-level mandates operate as independent wage obligations, categorized as "reporting time pay," which function as liquidated damages for labor-market instability rather than compensation for hours worked. Payroll controllers must distinguish these payments from standard wages, as reporting time pay does not constitute "hours worked" for the purpose of calculating the 40-hour federal overtime threshold under 29 C.F.R. § 778.216.

## California IWC Wage Orders: The Half-Day Liability Threshold

California industrial regulations mandate reporting time pay via the Industrial Welfare Commission (IWC) Wage Orders, specifically § 5 of each industry-specific order. Under these provisions, an employer must pay an employee who reports for a scheduled shift but is provided less than half the scheduled hours. The compensation requirement is defined as:

$$ \text{Reporting Pay} = \text{Regular Rate of Pay} \times \text{Greater of (2 Hours) or (1/2 Scheduled Shift)} $$

The "regular rate of pay" for these calculations must include all non-discretionary bonuses and differentials, as detailed in the [salary-exempt-vs-non-exempt-flsa-test](/guides/salary-exempt-vs-non-exempt-flsa-test/) guidelines. If an employee is scheduled for an eight-hour shift and is sent home after one hour, the employer is liable for four hours of pay. If an employee is scheduled for a three-hour shift and is sent home immediately, the employer is liable for two hours of pay. This mandate applies regardless of whether the employee remains on the premises, provided they presented themselves for work as scheduled. Failure to satisfy this liability often triggers additional penalties, including those associated with [rest-break-and-meal-period-penalty-pay-california](/guides/rest-break-and-meal-period-penalty-pay-california/), as the failure to pay reporting time constitutes an incomplete wage statement under Labor Code § 226.

## New York 12 NYCRR § 142-2.3: Minimum Wage Floor Application

New York Department of Labor regulation 12 NYCRR § 142-2.3 mandates reporting time pay for any employee who, by request or permission of the employer, reports for work on any shift. The statute dictates that the employee must be paid for at least three hours, or the number of hours in the regularly scheduled shift, whichever is less. Unlike California, which ties the rate to the employee's regular rate of pay, the New York regulation specifies payment at the "basic minimum hourly rate" applicable to that industry. 

When conducting a [reverse-biweekly-to-hourly-rate-audit](/guides/reverse-biweekly-to-hourly-rate-audit/), payroll controllers must isolate reporting time payments to ensure they are not erroneously treated as hours worked for overtime calculation. New York law provides a limited exception: if an employer can prove that the lack of work resulted from an "act of God" or another cause not within the employer's control, the reporting pay mandate is suspended. However, judicial interpretation of "outside employer control" remains narrow, typically excluding equipment failure or supply chain disruptions.

## Massachusetts Reporting Pay: The Three-Hour Trigger

Massachusetts law requires that any employee scheduled to work at least three hours who reports for duty must be paid for at least three hours at the state minimum wage or the employee's regular hourly rate, whichever is higher. This mandate (454 CMR 27.04) applies to all employees subject to the state’s minimum wage law. Unlike the California model, which scales based on the length of the scheduled shift, the Massachusetts requirement is a flat three-hour floor. 

Payroll systems must be calibrated to flag shifts where the "clock-in" time and "clock-out" time result in a total duration of less than three hours. If the discrepancy arises from an employer-initiated cancellation, the difference must be recorded as non-productive reporting time. This is distinct from [training-meetings-and-seminars-hours-worked-flsa](/guides/training-meetings-and-seminars-hours-worked-flsa/), where the time is compensable because it involves active labor or training.

## Overtime Exclusions and 29 C.F.R. § 778.216

A critical distinction in payroll accounting is that reporting time pay is not "compensation for hours worked" under the FLSA. Consequently, payments made for hours not worked—such as the two-hour premium paid when an employee is sent home after 30 minutes—cannot be credited toward the 40-hour workweek threshold. 

If an employee works 38 hours of actual productive labor and receives 2 hours of reporting time pay, the total compensation reflects 40 hours of pay, but the total "hours worked" remains 38. The employer is not obligated to pay overtime premiums on the reporting time portion, nor does that time count toward the trigger for weekly overtime. Conversely, if the reporting time pay is misclassified as "regular hours" in the payroll ledger, the automated system may incorrectly trigger an overtime premium. This creates a risk of wage-and-hour litigation under FLSA Section 16(b), where employees may sue for unpaid overtime based on the inflated "hours worked" reported on their pay stubs.

## Quantification of Employer Liability Under FLSA Section 16(b)

Employers who fail to accurately account for reporting time pay face significant exposure. Under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b), a prevailing plaintiff may recover unpaid wages, an equal amount in liquidated damages, and reasonable attorney's fees. The following table illustrates the potential financial liability for an employer failing to adhere to state-specific reporting time mandates for a single non-exempt employee earning $20.00/hour.

| State | Statutory Requirement | Hours Compensable | Total Liability (Wages) | Risk (Liquidated Damages) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **California** | 1/2 Scheduled Shift (Min 2) | 4 Hours (on 8hr shift) | $80.00 | $160.00 |
| **New York** | 3 Hours (at min wage) | 3 Hours | $60.00* | $120.00 |
| **Massachusetts** | 3 Hours (at min wage) | 3 Hours | $60.00* | $120.00 |

*\*Note: New York and Massachusetts calculations assume the state minimum wage is lower than or equal to the employee’s regular rate, with the liability floor set at the state-mandated minimum hourly rate for the reporting time portion.*

## Accounting Protocol for Reporting Time vs. Productive Hours

To maintain compliance with both federal and state standards, payroll controllers should implement a distinct "Reporting Time" earnings code. This code must be excluded from the "Total Hours Worked" field used to calculate the 40-hour overtime trigger. When reconciling pay periods, the total hours reported to the Department of Labor must be partitioned into "Actual Hours Worked" and "Reporting Time Pay." 

This separation is vital for audits, particularly when determining if the employer has met the [state-minimum-wage-annual-salary-rankings](/guides/state-minimum-wage-annual-salary-rankings/) requirements, as reporting time pay counts toward the minimum wage obligation but not toward the overtime threshold. Furthermore, when an employee is entitled to reporting time pay, the employer must ensure the payment is included in the "regular rate" calculation for any overtime earned in that same workweek, as the reporting pay is a form of remuneration for employment. 

## Regulatory Compliance and Recordkeeping Precision

Compliance failures often stem from the conflation of "reporting time" with "call-in pay" or "on-call time." Reporting time pay is strictly triggered by the act of presenting oneself at the worksite. If an employee is required to remain available via phone but is not required to be on-site, the state reporting time mandates generally do not apply. 

Internal audits should verify that the clock-in/clock-out software automatically generates the reporting time premium when a shift is ended prematurely by a supervisor override. Without this automation, the manual adjustments required to comply with California, New York, and Massachusetts statutes are prone to clerical error. Payroll controllers must maintain records of the "scheduled shift" versus the "actual shift" for a minimum of three years to satisfy the recordkeeping requirements of the FLSA and equivalent state labor codes. Failure to provide accurate, itemized wage statements that clearly delineate reporting time pay from hours worked serves as a primary driver for class-action litigation regarding wage theft and miscalculation of overtime.

Strict adherence to these jurisdictional nuances ensures that payroll operations remain shielded from the statutory penalties associated with FLSA Section 16(b) violations and state-specific labor code infractions.
