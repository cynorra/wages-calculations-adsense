Almost every travel-time dispute comes down to one question: **is the employee traveling to work, or traveling as part of work?** The first is a commute and unpaid. The second is working time that counts toward the 40-hour overtime threshold. The statute that draws the line is the Portal-to-Portal Act of 1947 (29 U.S.C. § 254), and the Department of Labor's interpretation lives in 29 C.F.R. §§ 785.33–785.41.

## Where the line comes from

Before 1947, courts had begun counting the time workers spent walking from the factory gate to their stations ("portal to portal"). Congress reversed that. Two kinds of activity are generally **not** compensable unless a contract or custom says otherwise:

1. Traveling to and from the actual place of performance of the principal activity.
2. Activities that are preliminary or postliminary to that principal activity.

Everything below is about the boundary of that exception: when travel *is* part of the principal activity, it is paid.

## Eight common scenarios

| Situation | Paid? | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Daily drive from home to the regular worksite | No | Ordinary home-to-work travel, 29 C.F.R. § 785.35 |
| Driving between two job sites during the workday | **Yes** | Travel that is "all in a day's work," § 785.38 |
| Reporting to the shop first, then driving a company truck to the site | **Yes** | The day's work began at the shop |
| Sent to another city for a single day and back the same day | **Yes**, minus the normal commute | Special one-day assignment, § 785.37 |
| Called back after hours for an emergency at a customer's site | **Yes**, from leaving home | Emergency call-out is outside normal commuting, § 785.36 |
| Overnight trip, traveling during normal work hours on a non-working day (e.g., Saturday 9–5) | **Yes** | Travel during normal hours is work time even on days off, § 785.39 |
| Overnight trip, traveling as a passenger after hours | No | Outside normal hours and not performing work |
| Overnight trip, employee is the **driver** after hours | **Yes** | Driving is work regardless of the hour |

## The special-assignment rule in practice

The most litigated scenario is a normally local employee sent far from home for one day. The DOL's position is that the *extra* travel beyond the ordinary commute is compensable. Suppose a technician with a 25-minute commute is dispatched to a site 2 hours from home:

$$\text{Paid travel each way} = 120 \text{ min} - 25 \text{ min} = 95 \text{ min}$$

Round trip, that is 190 minutes (3 h 10 min) of additional working time, which can push the week over 40 hours.

## Employer vehicles and the commute

Employees often drive a company vehicle home. Under the Employee Commuting Flexibility Act of 1996 (29 U.S.C. § 254(a)), using an employer-provided vehicle for the commute is not itself compensable if three conditions hold: the use is within the normal commuting area, it is subject to an agreement between the employer and employee or their representative, and the employee is not required to perform work during the commute beyond incidental tasks such as carrying tools. If the employee must take dispatch calls, pick up materials, or make customer stops on the way, those segments become work time.

## What counts as "normal working hours"

For overnight travel, hours of the workday are measured by the employee's regular schedule, and that includes **days off**. An employee who works Monday through Friday, 8 to 4:30, and is told to fly Saturday morning is working during normal hours even though Saturday is not a regular workday. A lunch stop that is a genuine meal period of 30 minutes or more can be deducted, but waiting at the airport while required to stay with equipment cannot.

## Pay rate for travel time

Travel time that is hours worked must be paid at no less than the minimum wage and counts toward the 40-hour overtime threshold. Where an employee earns different rates for different tasks in the same week, the overtime rate is usually a weighted average; see our guide on [dual-rate overtime](/guides/overtime-for-dual-rate-workers-blended-hourly-rate/).

## Payroll audit checklist

- Does your time system record the start of the first principal activity (loading the truck, first customer call), not just arrival at the first site?
- Are drives between sites captured, or only the time on site?
- For out-of-town trips, is travel on non-working days evaluated against the employee's normal hours?
- Do written travel policies match what supervisors actually do? Courts look at practice.
- Is a state rule stricter? California, for instance, treats time under the employer's control as hours worked, including required employer-provided transportation, under the IWC wage orders.

Back pay for unpaid travel is recoverable for two years, or three if the violation is willful, with an equal amount in liquidated damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b). The Portal-to-Portal Act itself provides a good-faith defense in § 259 where an employer relied in writing on a DOL interpretation.
