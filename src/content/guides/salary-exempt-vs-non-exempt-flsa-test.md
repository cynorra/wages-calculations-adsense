## The FLSA Three-Prong Exemption Test: Statutory Framework

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA), all employees are legally presumed to be **non-exempt**—meaning they are entitled to federal minimum wage and mandatory overtime pay of 1.5 times their regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in a workweek. 

To overcome this presumption and legally deny an employee overtime compensation under Section 13(a)(1) of the Act (**29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(1)**), an employer bears the legal burden of proving that the worker satisfies **all three prongs** of the federal exemption test codified in **29 C.F.R. Part 541**:

1. **The Salary Basis Test (29 C.F.R. § 541.602):** The employee must be paid a predetermined, fixed sum that is not subject to reduction based on variations in the quality or quantity of the work performed.
2. **The Salary Level Test (29 C.F.R. § 541.600):** The predetermined salary must meet or exceed the federally mandated statutory earnings threshold.
3. **The Standard Duties Test (29 C.F.R. §§ 541.100–541.304):** The employee's actual daily job functions—not their resume or formal job title—must primarily involve executive, administrative, professional, computer, or outside sales duties.

Failing any single prong automatically renders the employee non-exempt and fully entitled to overtime pay.

## Prong 1: The Salary Basis Rule and Impermissible Deductions

Under **29 C.F.R. § 541.602**, an exempt employee must receive their full salary for any week in which they perform any work, without regard to the number of days or hours worked:

> *"An employee is not paid on a salary basis if deductions from the predetermined compensation are made for absences occasioned by the employer or by the operating requirements of the business. If the employee is ready, willing, and able to work, deductions may not be made for time when work is not available."*

### What Employers CANNOT Deduct:
- Deducting pay because an office closed early on a snowy Friday.
- Deducting pay because the employee worked 36 hours instead of 40 hours in a given week.
- Deducting pay for poor work performance, missed production quotas, or cash register shortages.

### The Few Lawful Deductions Permitted:
- Absence from work for one or more full days for personal reasons, other than sickness or disability.
- Absence for one or more full days due to sickness or disability if deducted in accordance with a bona fide sick leave plan.
- Penalties imposed in good faith for infractions of safety rules of major significance.
- Unpaid disciplinary suspensions of one or more full days imposed in good faith for infractions of written workplace conduct rules.

**The "Window of Correction" Risk:** If an employer engages in an actual practice of making improper hourly deductions from a salaried worker's paycheck, the employer destroys the salary basis exemption for that employee—and potentially for all employees in the same job classification—retroactively reclassifying them as hourly non-exempt and owing massive back overtime pay.

## Prong 2: The Federal Salary Level Thresholds

Under Department of Labor regulations, the salary threshold serves as an objective screening mechanism to ensure that low-wage workers are not arbitrarily stripped of overtime protections:

| Exemption Classification | Regulatory Citation | Minimum Weekly Salary | Annualized Minimum Equivalent (2,080h) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Standard White-Collar Exemption** | 29 C.F.R. § 541.600 | $844.00 / week | **$43,888.00 / year** |
| **Highly Compensated Employee (HCE)**| 29 C.F.R. § 541.601 | $2,059.00 / week | **$107,432.00 / year** |
| **Computer Professional (Hourly Track)**| 29 C.F.R. § 541.400 | $27.63 / hour | **$57,470.40 / year** |

### State-Level Preemption (California, New York, Washington)
Several states enforce statutory salary thresholds substantially higher than the federal baseline:
- **California:** The state salary test requires at least twice the state minimum wage for full-time employment (currently over **$66,560 per year**).
- **New York:** Enforces regional salary thresholds exceeding **$62,000 per year** in New York City, Long Island, and Westchester County.
- **Washington State:** Uses an escalating multiplier of state minimum wage, requiring upwards of **$67,000+ per year**.

An employee earning $55,000 in California may satisfy federal tests but is **legally non-exempt under California law**, requiring 1.5x overtime for hours over 8 per day or 40 per week.

## Prong 3: The FLSA Duties Tests Detailed

Job titles are legally irrelevant under FLSA enforcement doctrine. An employer cannot convert an hourly cashier or receptionist into an exempt employee by titling them "Administrative Executive." Exemption depends entirely on their **primary duty** (the main or most important responsibility):

### 1. The Executive Exemption (29 C.F.R. § 541.100)
To qualify as an exempt executive, an employee must:
- Have a primary duty of managing the enterprise or a recognized department/subdivision.
- Customarily and regularly direct the work of **two or more full-time employees** (or the equivalent in part-time workers).
- Possess the authority to hire or fire employees, or their suggestions/recommendations regarding hiring, firing, advancement, or promotion must be given particular weight.

### 2. The Administrative Exemption (29 C.F.R. § 541.200)
The administrative exemption is the most litigated and misapplied classification in the United States. To qualify:
- The primary duty must consist of the performance of office or non-manual work directly related to the management or general business operations of the employer or employer's customers.
- The primary duty must include **the exercise of discretion and independent judgment with respect to matters of significance**.
- *Routine Clerical Work Disqualifies:* Preparing reports, entering data, scheduling meetings, or answering phones following established corporate templates does **not** involve independent judgment on matters of significance.

### 3. The Learned Professional Exemption (29 C.F.R. § 541.300)
To qualify as a learned professional:
- The primary duty must require advanced knowledge in a specialized field of science or learning.
- The knowledge must be customarily acquired by a prolonged course of specialized intellectual instruction (e.g., licensed attorneys, physicians, certified public accountants, registered nurses, licensed architects, and engineers).
- Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs), paralegals, and bookkeepers generally do not qualify and remain non-exempt.

## The Salaried Non-Exempt Category: Debunking the Salary-Overtime Myth

A widespread myth among workers and managers is that being paid a "salary" automatically forfeits overtime eligibility. This is false.

An employer can lawfully pay an employee on a salary basis while classifying them as **non-exempt**. In this structure:
- The employee receives a guaranteed weekly base salary (e.g., $1,000 per week for 40 scheduled hours).
- If the employee works 45 hours, the employer **must pay an additional 5 hours of overtime** at 1.5 times the regular rate ($37.50/hr = $187.50 extra), bringing the gross paycheck to $1,187.50.

## Misclassification Audits: Calculating Back Wages and Damages

When an employer misclassifies workers as exempt, the financial exposure under FLSA § 216(b) is severe:

### Audit Case Study: Retail Assistant Store Manager
- **Stated Salary:** $48,000 / year ($923.08 / week)
- **Actual Duties:** 85% spent stocking shelves, assisting retail customers, operating checkout lanes; zero hiring/firing authority.
- **Average Hours Worked:** 50 hours per week (10 overtime hours per week).
- **Statutory Regular Rate:** $\$923.08 / 40 \text{ hours} = \$23.08 \text{ per hour}$.
- **Overtime Rate (1.5x):** $\$23.08 \times 1.5 = \$34.62 \text{ per hour}$.
- **Weekly Unpaid Overtime:** $10 \text{ hours} \times \$34.62 = \mathbf{\$346.20 \text{ per week}}$.
- **Annual Back Wages:** $\$346.20 \times 50 \text{ weeks} = \mathbf{\$17,310.00}$.
- **Two-Year Audit Window Back Wages:** **$34,620.00**.
- **Mandatory 100% Liquidated Damages:** **+$34,620.00**.
- **Total Direct Payout Owed to One Employee:** **$69,240.00** (plus employer-paid attorney fees).

For a retail chain with 50 stores, this single misclassification pattern creates a **$3.4 million legal liability**.
