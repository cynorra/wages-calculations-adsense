"Hazard pay" gets used for several different things, and the confusion costs workers and employers money. Some people think the law guarantees extra pay for dangerous work. Some employers think a hazard premium sits outside overtime math. Both are wrong in the same direction. This guide separates what is required, what is voluntary, and what happens to the numbers once a premium exists.

## Myths and facts

**Myth: The FLSA requires extra pay for dangerous work.**
**Fact:** It does not. The Fair Labor Standards Act sets a minimum wage ($7.25 federally) and requires overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate after 40 hours. Nothing in it mandates a premium for danger, heat, height, or contagion. Hazard pay is a matter of contract, union agreement, employer policy, or—in a few cases—a specific statute or regulation.

**Myth: A hazard premium is a bonus and can be ignored when computing overtime.**
**Fact:** If the employer pays it as compensation for working under certain conditions, it is part of the regular rate. The DOL's regulation at 29 C.F.R. § 778.207(b) lists premium pay for "unpleasant or hazardous conditions" among payments that are included in the regular rate when they are made for the work itself. Only the premium paid for hours beyond the daily or weekly standard is excludable as an overtime premium under 29 U.S.C. § 207(e)(5)–(7).

**Myth: Danger means safety rules can be traded for money.**
**Fact:** OSHA's duty to provide a workplace free of recognized hazards applies regardless of any premium. Paying hazard pay does not satisfy, or replace, that duty.

## Where hazard pay is actually required

A handful of settings do require some form of danger or differential pay:

- **Federal civilian employees.** Under 5 U.S.C. § 5545(d), agencies pay a hazardous duty differential, set by regulation at 5 C.F.R. § 550.904, for specific duties such as work on high structures, with explosives, or with certain biological agents. Rates are percentages of the basic rate.
- **Military personnel.** Hazardous duty incentive pay and imminent danger pay are set by statute and by the Department of Defense.
- **Government contracts.** Some Service Contract Act and Davis-Bacon wage determinations list classifications that already include a hazard-related rate.
- **Union contracts.** Premiums for confined spaces, high-rise work, or hazardous materials are common in collective bargaining agreements.
- **State and local law.** A few jurisdictions have enacted temporary hazard-pay mandates for specific industries, usually grocery or health care during emergencies. These ordinances vary and often expire, so check current local law.

## Worked example: how a $3 premium changes overtime

A pipe-fitter earns **$30.00 per hour** and receives a **$3.00 per hour** premium for each hour worked in a confined space. This week the fitter works 46 hours, 20 of them in the confined space.

| Step | Calculation | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Straight-time pay for all hours | 46 × $30.00 | $1,380.00 |
| Hazard premium | 20 × $3.00 | $60.00 |
| Total straight-time compensation | $1,380.00 + $60.00 | $1,440.00 |
| Regular rate | $1,440.00 ÷ 46 | $31.30 |
| Half-time premium for 6 overtime hours | 6 × ($31.30 × 0.5) | $93.91 |
| Total pay | $1,440.00 + $93.91 | **$1,533.91** |

$$\text{Regular rate} = \frac{\$1{,}380.00 + \$60.00}{46} = \$31.30$$

An employer that calculated overtime at the base $30.00 would pay half-time of $15.00 × 6 = $90.00, a shortfall of $3.91 this week. Small per week, but it recurs for every worker and every week, and unpaid overtime carries equal liquidated damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b).

## Flat-sum hazard pay

When the premium is a flat sum instead of an hourly amount, the same principle applies: allocate it over the hours in the week it was earned. A $200 weekly hazard payment in a 50-hour week adds $4.00 per hour to the regular rate. Because the regular rate includes the full $200 for all 50 hours, the employee is owed an additional half-time on that $4.00 for each overtime hour.

## Documentation to keep

- A written description of the conditions that trigger the premium, so the amount attributable to each hour is clear.
- Time records that identify hours worked under the hazardous condition.
- The calculation of the regular rate for any week with overtime, kept for the three-year FLSA recordkeeping window under 29 C.F.R. Part 516.

## Regular Rate Treatment and Payroll Calculation Mechanics

Under 29 C.F.R. § 778.207(b), hazard pay differentials—whether paid as an hourly premium (e.g., an extra $4.00 per hour for confined space work) or as a flat lump-sum bonus per shift—must be included in the employee's regular rate of pay when computing overtime compensation. An employer cannot exclude hazard pay from the overtime calculation simply because it is labeled as hazardous duty compensation.

For example, consider an employee with a $20.00 base rate who earns a $5.00/hr hazard differential for 20 hours of a 50-hour workweek:

$\text{Straight-Time Earnings} = (50 \times \$20.00) + (20 \times \$5.00) = \$1{,}000.00 + \$100.00 = \$1{,}100.00$

$\text{Regular Rate} = \frac{\$1{,}100.00}{50 \text{ hours}} = \$22.00 \text{ per hour}$

$\text{Overtime Premium} = 10 \text{ OT hours} \times (0.5 \times \$22.00) = \$110.00$

$\text{Total Gross Pay} = \$1{,}100.00 + \$110.00 = \$1{,}210.00$

Excluding the hazard differential from the regular rate would reduce the overtime rate to $10.00/hr (producing only $100.00 OT), creating an unlawful FLSA wage underpayment under 29 U.S.C. § 207(a).
