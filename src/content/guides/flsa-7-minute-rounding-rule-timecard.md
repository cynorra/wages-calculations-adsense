## Federal Timekeeping Standards: 29 C.F.R. § 785.48 Authority

In hourly wage administration, precision in recording work time is governed by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) regulations codified at **29 C.F.R. § 785.48(b)**. Under federal law, employers are not required to pay employees down to the exact second, but any time-rounding convention utilized must adhere to strict regulatory standards:

> *"It has been found that in some industries, particularly where time clocks are used, there has been the practice for many years of recording employees' starting time and stopping time to the nearest 5 minutes, or to the nearest one-tenth or quarter of an hour. Presumably, this arrangement averages out so that the employees are fully paid for all the time they actually work... It will not result, over a period of time, in failure to compensate the employees properly for all the time they have actually worked."*

The most common implementation in American corporate payroll is the **quarter-hour (15-minute) rounding rule**, widely referred to as the **7-minute rule**.

## The 15-Minute / 7-Minute Rounding Matrix: Step-by-Step Breakdown

Under a 15-minute rounding system, an hour is divided into four 15-minute segments (:00, :15, :30, and :45). The mathematical midpoint of a 15-minute window is 7.5 minutes. Because clocks measure in whole minutes, federal enforcement policy establishes the 7-minute mark as the legal tipping point:

- **1 to 7 Minutes Past the Interval:** The clock punch **rounds down** to the preceding quarter hour.
- **8 to 14 Minutes Past the Interval:** The clock punch **rounds up** to the subsequent quarter hour.

### Complete 60-Minute Rounding Table

| Actual Clock-In / Clock-Out Punch | Rounded Time Recorded for Payroll | Direction of Adjustment | Time Adjustment Impact |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **8:00 AM – 8:07 AM** | **8:00 AM** | Rounds Down | Worker credited with up to 7 min extra (if arriving) |
| **8:08 AM – 8:15 AM** | **8:15 AM** | Rounds Up | Worker loses up to 7 min (if arriving) |
| **8:16 AM – 8:22 AM** | **8:15 AM** | Rounds Down | Worker credited with up to 7 min extra |
| **8:23 AM – 8:30 AM** | **8:30 AM** | Rounds Up | Worker loses up to 7 min |
| **8:31 AM – 8:37 AM** | **8:30 AM** | Rounds Down | Worker credited with up to 7 min extra |
| **8:38 AM – 8:45 AM** | **8:45 AM** | Rounds Up | Worker loses up to 7 min |
| **8:46 AM – 8:52 AM** | **8:45 AM** | Rounds Down | Worker credited with up to 7 min extra |
| **8:53 AM – 8:59 AM** | **9:00 AM** | Rounds Up | Worker credited up to 7 min (if departing) |

## The Legal Requirement of "Facial and Operational Neutrality"

The critical legal caveat under **29 C.F.R. § 785.48(b)** is that time-rounding schemes must be **neutral both on their face and in practical operation**. Rounding systems that systematically shave minutes in favor of the employer are illegal and constitute unlawful wage theft.

### Common Unlawful Rounding Implementations:

1. **One-Way Asymmetric Rounding:**
   - *Example:* An employer rounds an employee's morning arrival of 8:03 AM up to 8:15 AM (depriving the worker of 12 minutes of paid time), but when the worker leaves at 5:12 PM, the employer rounds the punch down to 5:00 PM (depriving the worker of another 12 minutes). Over a 5-day week, the employer steals two full hours of earned wages.
2. **Disciplinary Time Rounding:**
   - Docking an employee 15 minutes of pay for clocking in 1 minute late (e.g., punching at 8:01 AM and paying from 8:15 AM) while requiring the employee to immediately start working is illegal under FLSA. If the employee works from 8:01 AM, they must be compensated.
3. **Electronic Timekeeping Scrutiny (The Shift Away from Rounding):**
   - In recent appellate rulings (notably in the California Supreme Court case *Camp v. Home Depot USA, Inc.*), courts have questioned the necessity of 15-minute rounding in the era of digital biometric, GPS, and smartphone time clocks that record exact seconds. In states with strict wage-hour laws, employers are increasingly required to pay to the exact minute.

## Meal and Rest Periods: Bona Fide Breaks vs. Compensable Hours

A frequent area of timecard manipulation involves meal break deductions. Federal standards for breaks are governed by **29 C.F.R. § 785.18 and § 785.19**:

### 1. Short Rest Periods (5 to 20 Minutes) — ALWAYS COMPENSABLE
Under **29 C.F.R. § 785.18**, short rest periods of 20 minutes or less (such as coffee breaks or restroom visits) are customary in commercial enterprises and **must be counted as hours worked**. An employer cannot require workers to clock out to use the restroom or take a 10-minute rest break.

### 2. Bona Fide Meal Periods (30+ Minutes) — UNPAID ONLY IF COMPLETELY RELIEVED
Under **29 C.F.R. § 785.19**, meal periods are not worktime and are unpaid only if the employee is **completely relieved from duty**:

> *"The employee is not relieved if he is required to perform any duties, whether active or inactive, while eating. For example, an office employee who is required to eat at his desk or a factory worker who is required to be at his machine is working while eating."*

### Auto-Deduct Software Traps:
Many corporate payroll platforms automatically deduct 30 minutes from daily timecards for an assumed lunch break. If a nurse, warehouse worker, or dispatcher continues monitoring radios, answering patient call buttons, or responding to client emails during lunch, the automatic 30-minute deduction represents **unlawful wage withholding**.

## Practical Example: Weekly Timecard Audit

Consider an hourly dental hygienist earning **$35.00 per hour** who punches the following timecard across a 5-day week under a 15-minute rounding policy:

| Day | Actual Morning Punch | Rounded In | Actual Evening Punch | Rounded Out | Total Paid Hours | Net Rounding Gain/Loss |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Monday** | 7:54 AM | 8:00 AM | 5:06 PM | 5:00 PM | 8.00 Hours | -12 Minutes (Worker lost) |
| **Tuesday** | 8:06 AM | 8:00 AM | 4:55 PM | 5:00 PM | 8.00 Hours | +11 Minutes (Worker gained) |
| **Wednesday**| 7:58 AM | 8:00 AM | 5:08 PM | 5:15 PM | 8.25 Hours | +5 Minutes (Worker gained) |
| **Thursday** | 8:02 AM | 8:00 AM | 5:01 PM | 5:00 PM | 8.00 Hours | +1 Minute (Worker gained) |
| **Friday** | 7:53 AM | 8:00 AM | 4:54 PM | 5:00 PM | 8.00 Hours | -1 Minute (Worker lost) |
| **TOTAL** | — | — | — | — | **40.25 Hours** | **+4 Minutes Net Gain for Worker** |

In this scenario, because the rounding swings in both directions across the week, the system satisfies the federal operational neutrality test. However, if an internal audit demonstrates that over a 6-month period the net rounding variance consistently trends negative (depriving the workforce of cumulative hours), the employer faces collective action liability.

## How Workers Can Protect Themselves Against Timecard Shaving

To ensure lawful compensation under federal wage standards:

1. **Maintain Personal Time Logs:** Use a personal smartphone notes app or time-tracking software to record exact clock-in, clock-out, and lunch durations independently of company records.
2. **Review Paystub Hours Against Schedule:** Verify that every paystub explicitly lists total straight-time hours, overtime hours, and gross pay matching actual hours worked.
3. **Flag Unpaid Interrupted Lunches:** If your lunch break was interrupted by work tasks, immediately submit a time adjustment form requesting pay for the full meal period.
4. **File Inquiries with the DOL:** Non-exempt workers facing systematic time shaving can file confidential inquiries with the US Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division (WHD) at 1-866-4US-WAGE without fear of legal employer retaliation under FLSA § 15(a)(3).
