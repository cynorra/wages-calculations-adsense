## Under what conditions does the FLSA permit excluding training time from compensable hours?

Under 29 C.F.R. § 785.27, an employer may exclude time spent in lectures, meetings, and training programs from an employee’s compensable hours only if four specific criteria are satisfied concurrently. Failure to meet any one of these requirements renders the time compensable under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). First, attendance must occur outside the employee’s regular working hours. Second, attendance must be entirely voluntary; the employer cannot exert pressure, explicit or implicit, suggesting that non-attendance will result in adverse employment consequences. Third, the training must not be directly related to the employee’s current job. Fourth, the employee must not perform any productive work during the training session. If the employer mandates attendance, or if the training is necessary for the employee to maintain their current position, the time constitutes "hours worked." Payroll controllers must distinguish between voluntary professional development and employer-directed training. When calculating the [real hourly pay rate after tax](/guides/real-hourly-pay-rate-after-tax/), these hours must be aggregated into the total workweek to determine if the employee exceeds the 40-hour threshold triggering overtime pay under 29 U.S.C. § 207(a).

## How does the Department of Labor define "directly related" to an employee's job?

The determination of whether training is "directly related" to an employee's job, as defined in 29 C.F.R. § 785.29, hinges on whether the content is designed to improve the employee's performance in their current position. Training is considered directly related if it teaches techniques or processes currently utilized by the employer. Conversely, training is not directly related if it is designed to prepare the employee for a different job, advancement to a new position, or the development of skills that are not required for the current role. For example, if an employer provides a voluntary course on data analytics for a warehouse clerk whose job description involves manual inventory tracking, the time is likely non-compensable. However, if the employer mandates the training to ensure the clerk can utilize the company’s new inventory software, the training is directly related to the current role and must be compensated. Payroll audits must verify that training is not a prerequisite for retaining the existing job, as mandated training is always compensable regardless of its classification as "professional development."

## Are employer-mandated meetings held outside of standard shifts compensable?

Any meeting required by an employer is compensable time under 29 C.F.R. § 785.27. If an employer requires staff to attend a morning briefing or an evening safety lecture, the time spent in attendance qualifies as hours worked. This remains true even if the meeting takes place before the start of the scheduled shift or after the employee has clocked out. Because the meeting is mandatory, it fails the "voluntary" test mandated by the regulation, thereby nullifying the possibility of excluding it from the payroll. Controllers must ensure these hours are captured in the timekeeping system to avoid violations of the FLSA. If the meeting time pushes the employee’s total hours beyond 40 in a workweek, the employer must pay the overtime premium of at least 1.5 times the regular rate, as defined in 29 C.F.R. § 778.107. Failure to record these minutes often leads to significant back-pay liabilities during [salary exempt vs non-exempt FLSA test](/guides/salary-exempt-vs-non-exempt-flsa-test/) audits, as the Department of Labor views these sessions as essential operational components.

## Does the performance of productive work during a training session mandate compensation?

The performance of any productive work during a training session automatically disqualifies that time from the exclusion criteria set forth in 29 C.F.R. § 785.27. If an employee is attending a seminar but is simultaneously tasked with answering customer emails, processing payroll data, or monitoring production lines, the training session is converted into hours worked. The Department of Labor’s position is that the presence of productive work indicates the employee is serving the employer’s interest, regardless of the educational setting. Payroll controllers should exercise caution when employees participate in webinars or virtual training while logged into internal systems. If an employee is expected to "multitask" during a training video, the entire duration of that video must be recorded as compensable time. This regulation prevents employers from masking productive labor as non-compensable "educational time." When performing a [reverse biweekly to hourly rate audit](/guides/reverse-biweekly-to-hourly-rate-audit/), all such "dual-purpose" time must be included in the denominator to ensure the calculated hourly rate does not fall below the [state minimum wage annual salary rankings](/guides/state-minimum-wage-annual-salary-rankings/).

## How do legal or regulatory requirements for certification affect compensation?

Training that is a legal requirement for the maintenance of a professional license or certification is generally considered compensable if it is required by the employer to hold the position. Under 29 C.F.R. § 785.30, if the employer requires the training to keep the job, the time is compensable. However, the FLSA distinguishes between training required by the employer and training required by an outside licensing board for the individual's own professional credentials. If an employer mandates that a nurse obtain a specific certification to remain in their role, the training time is compensable. If the state requires a license for the profession, but the employee chooses to maintain that license independently of the specific employer's demand, the time may be non-compensable, provided the other criteria of 29 C.F.R. § 785.27 are met. Payroll departments must document the nexus between the certification and the specific job requirements. If the training is a condition of continued employment, the "voluntary" element is absent, rendering the training hours compensable regardless of when they occur.

## How is travel time to training sessions handled under the FLSA?

Travel time to training is governed by the principles established in the Portal-to-Portal Act, specifically 29 C.F.R. § 785.37. If an employee is required to travel to a training site away from their home community, the time spent traveling during the employee's normal working hours is compensable. This rule applies on non-working days (such as Saturday or Sunday) as well as regular workdays. If the training occurs during the employee's normal working hours, travel time is deemed hours worked. However, if an employee travels outside of normal working hours—for example, catching a flight in the evening—the time spent in transit is generally not compensable unless the employee is performing productive work during the travel. Payroll controllers must distinguish between "home-to-work" travel, which is typically not compensable, and "travel to a special assignment," which is compensable. For further clarity on the nuances of transit, refer to the [travel time pay portal to portal act](/guides/travel-time-pay-portal-to-portal-act/) guidelines to ensure compliance with federal transit compensation standards.

## Summary of Compensability Criteria

The following table summarizes the requirements for excluding training time from payroll. If any condition is checked "No," the time must be compensated.

| Criterion | Requirement for Non-Compensability |
| :--- | :--- |
| Timing | Must occur outside regular working hours |
| Voluntariness | Attendance must be strictly voluntary |
| Relevance | Must not be directly related to the current job |
| Productivity | No productive work performed during session |

If the training is mandatory, the employer must compensate the employee for the duration of the session at the applicable hourly rate. If the employee is non-exempt, this time contributes to the 40-hour overtime threshold. Employers must ensure that internal timekeeping software accurately reflects these hours to prevent claims for unpaid wages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b). Precision in documenting the nature of training sessions is the primary defense against wage-and-hour litigation in the current regulatory environment. Payroll controllers and business owners should maintain detailed records of all training curriculum and attendance logs to substantiate the classification of training time as non-compensable when applicable. Adherence to these strict definitions ensures the integrity of the payroll cycle and compliance with federal labor standards.
