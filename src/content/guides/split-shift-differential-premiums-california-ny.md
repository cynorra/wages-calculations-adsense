A split shift divides one workday into two or more working periods with an unpaid gap between them: a restaurant server who works the lunch rush, goes home for three hours, and returns for dinner. The FLSA treats the gap as non-working time as long as the employee is completely free to use it, and it has no premium for the inconvenience. Two large states, however, require extra pay. The rules look similar but are triggered differently, and employers who operate in both can easily apply the wrong one.

## Side by side

| | California | New York |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Name | Split shift premium | Spread of hours |
| Source | IWC Wage Orders, § 4(C) | 12 N.Y.C.R.R. §§ 142-2.4 and 146-1.6 |
| Trigger | Work schedule interrupted by an unpaid, non-meal period set by the employer | Workday longer than **10 hours** from start to finish, counting the gap and meal time |
| Premium | One hour at the **minimum wage** | One hour at the **basic minimum hourly rate** |
| Who | Employees under the wage orders, with an offset for those paid above minimum wage | In most industries, employees paid at the minimum wage; the hospitality wage order is broader |
| Gap length | Any unpaid non-working period other than a bona fide meal or rest break | Any, as long as total span is over 10 hours |

## California: the earnings offset

The premium is one hour of pay at the **applicable minimum wage**. But California courts have held that it is owed only when the employee's total pay for the day does not already exceed the minimum wage for all hours worked **plus** the premium hour. Employees who earn well above minimum wage often do not trigger it.

With a $16.90 minimum wage, consider an employee paid $17.00 per hour who works 4 hours in the morning and 3 hours in the evening, 7 hours total:

$$\text{Pay earned} = 7 \times \$17.00 = \$119.00$$

$$\text{Required minimum with premium} = (7 \times \$16.90) + \$16.90 = \$135.20$$

Because $119.00 is less than $135.20, the employee is owed the shortfall, which is **$16.20**. If the employee were paid $20.00 per hour, earnings of $140.00 would exceed $135.20 and no premium would be owed.

The wage order exempts employees who live at the place of employment. Local minimum wages that are higher than the state's raise the premium accordingly, since the premium uses the minimum wage that applies where the work is performed.

### Reporting-time pay is separate

California also requires **reporting time pay** (Wage Order § 5) when an employee reports and is given less than half a scheduled shift: pay for half the usual or scheduled day, but at least 2 hours and at most 4 hours at the regular rate. A split-shift employee sent home early may be owed both.

## New York: the spread of hours

The spread of hours is the length of time between the beginning and end of the workday, **including** time off for meals and any interval between split shifts. If it exceeds 10 hours, the employee receives one additional hour at the basic minimum hourly rate.

With a New York City minimum wage of $17.00, take a worker who clocks in at 7:00 a.m., works until 11:00 a.m., and returns from 4:00 p.m. to 9:00 p.m.:

$$\text{Spread of hours} = 7{:}00 \text{ a.m.} \text{ to } 9{:}00 \text{ p.m.} = 14 \text{ hours}$$

$$\text{Hours worked} = 4 + 5 = 9 \qquad \text{Pay} = 9 \times \$17.00 + \$17.00 = \$170.00$$

In industries covered by Part 142, spread-of-hours pay applies to employees paid at the minimum wage. In hospitality, the Part 146 wage order extends it more broadly, so check the correct wage order before concluding an employee is exempt from the premium. New York also has **call-in pay** rules, typically requiring pay for a minimum number of hours when an employee reports for a shift.

## Overtime interaction

Both premiums are paid at the minimum wage rate rather than the employee's actual rate, and the premium hour is **not** time worked. Count overtime on the hours actually worked, not on the premium hour. Whether a given premium also feeds into the regular rate depends on how your policy and the applicable wage order define it, so confirm that point before building it into payroll.

## Payroll steps

- Pull time punches by day and by employee and flag gaps, not just total hours.
- In California, compare daily earnings to minimum wage times hours plus one hour.
- In New York, measure the first clock-in to the last clock-out, including meal periods.
- Show the premium on its own line in the wage statement.
- Write the policy per state. A multi-state employer should not use one shared "split shift" rule.
